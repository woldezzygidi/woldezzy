Yes, Google keeps more clicks on its own pages than it used to, and AI Overviews and AI Mode have made that worse for many sites. But "stealing" is the wrong frame if you want to do anything useful about it. The click from Google was never owed to you. What has changed is how much of the answer Google now gives away before anyone reaches your page, and that changes what a good SEO strategy looks like.

I first wrote this in June 2022, after a Semrush Twitter Space with Aleyda Solís on exactly this question. Back then the argument was about featured snippets and the Knowledge Graph, and my position was that we expect too much from Google. Four years on, Google has put a generative answer above the results in most of the world, launched a separate AI search mode, and become the first company the UK's competition regulator has formally designated with strategic market status in search. I still think Google is not your enemy. I no longer think the deal is as fair as I made it sound.

What I argued in 2022, and what still holds

My original point was simple. Google's job is to answer the searcher's question as smoothly as possible. Publishers enjoy a free flow of traffic from Google, and it is easy to forget that the traffic is a by-product of Google serving its users, not the purpose. When featured snippets started answering questions on the results page, lyrics sites and quick-answer sites were hit hardest. Genius famously accused Google of lifting its lyrics. I said then that it hurt to watch, but that there was nothing unfair in principle about Google showing a short answer.

That part still holds. If your entire business model is restating a fact that fits in one sentence, Google was always going to take that query from you, and so will every AI assistant.

What does not hold any more is the scale. A featured snippet quoted one source and linked to it prominently. An AI Overview writes its own answer from several sources and puts the links in a side panel or under a "show more". AI Mode goes further and turns the search into a conversation where the blue links are an afterthought. That is a different bargain, and it deserves a more honest answer than "keep striving".

What are AI Overviews and AI Mode, and when did they arrive?

AI Overviews are the generated summaries that appear above the organic results for many queries. Google launched them in the US in May 2024 and brought them to the UK, India, Japan, Indonesia, Mexico and Brazil on 15 August 2024. By 2025 they were showing in more than 200 countries and territories.

AI Mode is a separate, chat-style search experience reached through its own tab. It went live for US users in May 2025 and launched in the UK at the end of July 2025. In August 2025 Google expanded it in English to around 180 more countries. At Google I/O in May 2026 the company redesigned the search box itself around longer, conversational questions, and said AI Mode had passed a billion monthly users.

For anyone in the UK, the practical point is that both features have been live here for over a year. If your traffic changed in late 2024 or the second half of 2025 and you never worked out why, this is the first place to look.

What Google says about clicks and traffic

Google's public position is that AI in Search is good for the web. In August 2025 Liz Reid, head of Google Search, published a post titled "AI in Search is driving more queries and higher quality clicks". The claims were that total organic click volume from Google to websites had been relatively stable year on year, that people were searching more because AI made complex questions easier to ask, and that the clicks that did happen were "higher quality", meaning people were less likely to bounce straight back.

Notice what that statement does and does not say. "Relatively stable" in aggregate can hide a lot of winners and losers. Google did not publish the underlying data, and an aggregate figure across the whole web tells you nothing about your site. Some kinds of site may be gaining while others lose, and the average stays flat.

What publishers and researchers actually measure

The independent numbers point the other way. In July 2025 Pew Research Center analysed the real browsing behaviour of US adults and found that people clicked a traditional search result on 8% of visits where an AI summary appeared, against 15% where none appeared. Clicks on the links inside the summary itself were rare, around 1% of visits.

Publishers' own reporting has been consistent with that. News, reference and how-to sites have complained loudly, and their trade bodies took the complaints to regulators on both sides of the Channel. I would treat any single third-party CTR study with caution, because methodology varies a lot. But when real-user panel data, publisher analytics and the direction of regulation all agree, I do not need a precise number to believe the direction.

On client sites I work on, the pattern is uneven rather than uniform. Informational pages that answered simple questions lost the most clicks, often while impressions held steady or rose. That is the signature of zero-click search: Google shows your page more and sends you fewer visitors. Pages tied to a decision, a purchase, a booking or a specific local need held up much better. I wrote about how that breaks attribution in the dark funnel post, because a falling click line does not always mean falling demand.

What regulators have done: the CMA and the European Commission

This is the part that did not exist when I wrote the original, and it matters more than any single study.

In the UK, the Competition and Markets Authority designated Google with strategic market status in general search and search advertising on 10 October 2025, under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act. That lets the CMA impose legally binding conduct requirements. In early June 2026 it imposed the first one, aimed at publishers: Google must give them effective tools to stop their content being used to power AI features in search, attribute their content with clear links, and report on compliance. Google has nine months to put all of it in place. On 17 June 2026 the CMA added a fair ranking requirement, which says Google must rank organic results, including AI Overviews, on objective and non-discriminatory criteria and give businesses more notice of significant changes.

In the EU, the European Commission opened a formal antitrust investigation on 9 December 2025 into whether Google used publishers' content and YouTube videos for AI Overviews and AI Mode without fair compensation, and whether publishers can refuse without losing their place in Google Search. That investigation is ongoing as I write.

Google's direct response was a new setting. The Search generative AI control in Search Console lets a site exclude its content from AI Overviews, AI Mode and the AI features in Discover. It launched for a group of UK sites in June 2026, and Google says it has been available to all websites worldwide since 31 August 2026. Google states that excluding your site does not affect ranking in regular results. Alongside it came a generative AI performance report showing impressions in those features.

Should you opt out of AI Overviews?

For most businesses, no. Opting out removes you from the AI answer entirely. You get no impressions and no clicks from those features, and your competitors' content gets used instead. If AI Overviews appear on your important queries, the people who read them still form an opinion of the market, and you will not be in it.

There are cases where it makes sense to test it:

  • Your content is the product. Original reporting, data, reference material or paid research that the AI summary reproduces well enough that nobody needs your page.
  • Your search console data shows high AI impressions on pages that used to convert, alongside a clear drop in clicks to those pages.
  • You have a licensing or commercial reason to withhold content from AI systems and want the legal position to be clear.

Even then, I would run it as a test. The control works at the property level, so a separate subdomain or Search Console property can let you try it on one section first. Measure for several weeks before you decide. And remember that opting out of Google's AI features does nothing about ChatGPT, Perplexity or Claude, which read the web differently. I covered those differences in the fault lines post.

What publishers and businesses can do about zero-click search

This is what I actually tell clients at SEOGidi. None of it depends on Google being fair.

  1. Measure the right thing. Pull clicks and impressions from Search Console by page and query type, then check the generative AI performance report. Rising impressions with falling clicks on informational pages is zero-click. Falling impressions is a ranking problem. They need different fixes.
  2. Sort your pages by intent. Pages that answer a quick fact are now awareness assets, not traffic assets. Pages that help someone compare, decide, buy, book or contact you are where the clicks still are. Put your effort there.
  3. Give people a reason to click. Original data, tools, calculators, templates, case detail, local specifics and opinion from someone with real experience. An AI summary can paraphrase a definition. It cannot hand over your spreadsheet or your judgement.
  4. Aim to be cited as well as ranked. AI Overviews link to sources. Clear structure, direct answers near the top, named authors, and facts that match what reputable third parties say about you all improve your odds of being one of those links. This is entity work, which I explain in entities, not keywords.
  5. Build demand Google cannot intercept. Branded search, email lists, communities, YouTube, podcasts and direct relationships. If people search for you by name, there is no AI summary standing between you and the click.
  6. Report on outcomes. If leads and revenue hold while sessions fall, the business is fine. Stop letting a sessions chart decide whether SEO is working.

So, is Google going too far?

My honest answer has moved. In 2022 I said Google is not stealing your traffic and that we expect too much from it. I still believe Google's first duty is to the searcher, and I still think many of the sites that lost most were thin businesses built on a quirk of the old results page.

But the UK regulator has now found that Google has enough power in search to need binding rules, and the first rule it wrote was about publishers and AI. That tells you the bargain had tipped. The new controls and reporting are a direct result of that pressure, not of Google's goodwill. I think that is a fair outcome. Google keeps the right to build better answers, and publishers get a real choice and real data about what those answers cost them.

Google is still not your enemy. It is also not your partner. Treat it as a channel you do not control, take what it gives, and build the parts of your marketing that do not depend on it.

Quick answers

Do AI Overviews reduce clicks?
On average, yes. Pew's 2025 study found people clicked a result on 8% of searches with an AI summary against 15% without. Google says total clicks are stable, but has not published data to show it.

Is AI Mode available in the UK?
Yes. AI Mode launched in the UK in late July 2025, and AI Overviews have been here since August 2024.

Can I stop Google using my content in AI Overviews?
Yes. The Search generative AI control in Search Console excludes your site from AI Overviews, AI Mode and Discover's AI features. Google says it does not affect your normal rankings.

Does blocking Google-Extended remove me from AI Overviews?
No. Google-Extended covers use of your content for Gemini models, not Search features. Use the Search Console control or snippet controls such as nosnippet instead.
