In April 2020 I wrote a short post telling bloggers and content creators what to publish during the COVID-19 lockdown: DIY and how-to posts, live sessions, online classes, interviews and interactive posts. Those ideas worked at the time. The lasting lesson was bigger than any one idea. When demand shifts suddenly, the people who notice first and publish fastest win the attention, and the people who keep running last month's calendar lose it.

Lockdown is over, so a list of lockdown content ideas is not much use to anyone now. I have kept a short record of what I wrote and what happened, because it is part of this site's history. The rest of this post is the playbook I use today when a sudden shift in demand hits a client's market, whether that is a pandemic, a price shock, a new regulation, a product recall or a story that blows up overnight.

What I recommended for lockdown content in April 2020

The UK went into lockdown on 23 March 2020. In Nigeria, President Buhari announced a stay-at-home order for Lagos, Abuja and Ogun State that took effect on 30 March. I was working across both audiences when I wrote the original post, about three weeks in. The argument was simple: huge numbers of people were at home, online, and looking for something to fill the time, so content creators should be publishing for them.

These were my five suggestions, as I wrote them then.

  1. Do-it-yourself posts. People wanted to learn something during lockdown: fashion tips, makeup, cooking, whatever. On the site I was running, DIY posts were outperforming everything else we had published within their first day.
  2. Go live on social media. People were spending hours watching live video. I pointed to Ubi Franklin of Triple MG going live on Instagram to interview successful people about their stories.
  3. Organise online classes. Plenty of people had time and wanted to learn, so it was a good moment to sell what you know.
  4. Conduct interviews. Busy people were suddenly less busy. Founders and executives who would normally ignore a request were saying yes.
  5. Make interactive posts. Polls, questions, opinion posts. You had your audience's attention, so ask them things.

What actually worked, looking back

Most of it held up. The how-to idea was the strongest, and the wider data backs it. Google's Year in Search for 2020 was full of how-to queries: how to make hand sanitiser, how to cut hair at home, bread recipes. April 2020 even saw a record spike in people searching "what day is it?". People were searching for practical help with a life that had changed overnight.

Live video and interviews worked too, but the return was short. Everyone went live at once, and by early summer the novelty had faded. The interviews were the better investment, because they produced content that could be cut, transcribed and republished long after the live audience had moved on.

What I did not say clearly enough in 2020 was which of that content would last. A lot of lockdown content was tied to the moment. "Ten things to do in lockdown" was useful for a few months and then became dead weight on the site. The DIY posts that taught a real skill, like a recipe or a home workout, kept getting traffic for years because the underlying need never went away. That difference between content tied to the shock and content that serves the need behind it is the core of everything below.

What is a demand shock in content and SEO?

A demand shock is a sudden change in what people search for, buy or need, caused by something outside your control. It can go up or down. Lockdown sent searches for home workouts and video calls through the roof, and sent travel searches through the floor in the same week.

Smaller versions happen all the time. A change in tax rules, an energy price rise, a viral TikTok, a competitor going bust, a heatwave, a product recall. For a business in the right category, each of those changes the questions customers ask, sometimes for a week, sometimes for good.

Two things are different now compared with 2020. First, AI Overviews and AI Mode answer a lot of simple news-style questions directly in Google, so the easy traffic from a breaking question is smaller than it was. I covered how the different AI platforms pick sources in the fault lines post. Second, the monitoring tools are much faster, which means you have less excuse for noticing late.

How to spot a demand shift early

You need two views: what the world is searching for, and what people are searching for when they find your site.

Google Trends

Use Trending Now for breaking topics. Since its August 2024 update it refreshes every ten minutes on average, covers 125 countries, and lets you filter by the last four hours, 24 hours, 48 hours or seven days. Use Explore to compare your core topics over time and to spot rising related queries. Save a short list of the five to ten topics that matter most to your business and check them weekly.

Google Search Console

Search Console tells you what is changing for your own site. Google added a 24 hours view to the Performance report in December 2024, with hourly data that you can export, so you can see a spike the same day rather than a week later.

What to watch:

  • New queries. Compare the last seven days with the previous seven and sort by impressions difference. Queries that appear from nowhere are the early signal.
  • Impressions rising without clicks. People are searching, Google is showing you, but your page does not answer what they want now. That is a page to update.
  • Sudden falls on core pages. Demand for that topic may have dropped, or the meaning of the query may have changed.

Your own front line

Sales calls, support tickets, social comments and enquiry forms often show a shift before search data does. Ask the people who talk to customers what changed this week. It costs nothing.

How to publish fast when demand shifts

Speed matters, but publishing something thin quickly is worse than publishing something useful a day later. This is the process I use.

  1. Confirm the shift is real. Check it in at least two places: Trends and Search Console, or Search Console and your inbox. One noisy spike is not a strategy.
  2. Write down the new question. In the customer's words. "Can I still get a refund if my flight is cancelled?" is a better brief than "travel disruption content".
  3. Update before you create. If you already have a page that ranks for the topic, add a clearly dated section at the top that answers the new question. Existing pages with history are indexed and trusted faster than new URLs.
  4. Create a new page only when the question is new. Give it a plain, specific title and answer the question in the first paragraph.
  5. Link to it from where people already land. Your homepage, the relevant category page and the top two or three pages getting traffic for related terms.
  6. Push it out on social and email the same day. Search takes time. Your own channels do not. The community post covers why owned audiences matter most in moments like this.
  7. Set a review date. Put a date in the calendar, usually two to four weeks out, to check whether the page is still needed.

Evergreen or news content: which should you publish?

Usually both, in that order of priority.

News content answers what is happening now. It gets a burst of attention and then fades. It is worth doing if you can be genuinely early or if you have a view nobody else has. If you are the fortieth site rewriting the same announcement, AI Overviews and the big publishers will take that traffic, and you will not.

Evergreen content answers the need underneath the news. During lockdown the news was "gyms are closed". The evergreen need was "how do I exercise at home". The second kept bringing in traffic for years. For most businesses, that is the better use of limited time.

A simple test: will someone search this exact question in a year's time? If yes, build it properly and keep it updated. If no, keep it short, date it clearly, and be ready to merge or retire it.

When to pause content, and when not to

Sometimes the right move is to stop publishing. In March 2020 a lot of planned content suddenly looked tone-deaf. Travel brands scheduling holiday inspiration and event companies promoting sold-out dates had to pull posts.

Pause or rework scheduled content when:

  • it promotes something customers cannot currently buy or use
  • its tone clashes badly with what people are going through
  • it gives advice that the new situation has made wrong

Do not take the website down, though. Google's guidance on temporarily pausing a business online, first published in March 2020 and still current, says to limit functionality rather than disable the site: turn off the cart, add a banner explaining the situation, and update structured data and business hours. Taking a whole site offline can cost rankings that take a long time to recover. A 503 status code is only for very short outages of a day or two.

How to clean up after the shock passes

This is the step almost everyone skips. After 2020, many sites I audited were still carrying dozens of lockdown posts nobody read, some with advice that had become wrong.

  1. List every page you created or changed during the shift.
  2. For each one, check Search Console clicks over the last three months.
  3. Keep and refresh pages that still get traffic and still answer a live need.
  4. Merge thin or overlapping pages into the strongest one, with a 301 redirect.
  5. Remove dated banners and sections from evergreen pages once they no longer apply.
  6. Write down what worked, so the next shock starts from notes rather than from scratch.

That last step is the reason I kept the 2020 record above. The specific ideas were for a moment. The habit of watching demand, answering the new question quickly and cleaning up afterwards is what I would want a client to take from it. Quick answers

Are lockdown content ideas still useful?
As a list, not really. As a pattern, yes. How-to content that solves a practical problem a sudden change creates is still the most reliable thing to publish in any demand shock.

How quickly should I publish when a trend appears?
Within a day or two if you can add something useful. Update an existing page first, because it will be picked up faster than a new URL.

Should I delete old COVID-19 content?
Not automatically. Check its traffic. Keep what still helps people, merge what overlaps, and redirect anything you remove to the closest relevant page.

Set up a Search Console comparison of the last seven days against the previous seven, and look at it every Monday. That one habit would have caught most of the demand shifts I have seen since 2020.
