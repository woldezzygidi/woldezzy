How to Get Organic Traffic for Free: My Content Playbook for Earning Visits Without Ad Spend

By 'Wole Oduwole

The fastest free organic traffic usually comes from pages you already have, not new ones. Find the queries Google already shows you for but does not yet rank you well for, improve the pages sitting just off page one, link to them properly, and only then write new content aimed at questions your competitors have ignored. Every step below uses free tools.

The original version of this post, from 2022, was written about me in the third person, as if someone else were reviewing my methods. It read like it, and it was mostly general advice about posting consistently and engaging on social media. This is the version I would actually give a founder who has a website, a few months of data and no ad budget. If you are starting from zero, read my guide to basic SEO techniques first, because none of this works on a site Google cannot index.

Where does free organic traffic actually come from?

Three places, in order of how quickly they pay off:

  1. Pages that already rank, but badly. Google has decided they are relevant. You only need to convince it they are the best answer.
  2. Queries Google already associates with you that no page on your site properly answers.
  3. New queries with low competition that sit inside your expertise.

Most people start with the third and wonder why nothing happens for six months. Start with the first.

How to find low-competition queries with free tools

You do not need a paid keyword tool to find opportunities. You need patience and four free sources.

Google Search Console

This is your best keyword tool once your site has a few months of data, because it shows real queries for your real pages. Open the Performance report, set the date range to the last three months, and tick average CTR and average position.

  1. Sort by impressions. Look for queries with high impressions and few clicks. Those are searches where you are visible but not chosen.
  2. Use a custom regex query filter to isolate questions. Something like ^(how|what|why|when|which|can|does|is|should)\s will show question queries you appear for.
  3. Click into a page, then the Queries tab, to see every query that page appears for. Often a page ranks for things you never targeted. Those are clues to sections you should add.
  4. Export to Google Sheets so you can filter and keep a record.

Google autocomplete and People Also Ask

Type your main topic into Google and stop before you finish. The suggestions are real searches. Add a letter after your topic ("bookkeeping for a", "bookkeeping for b") to see more. Then look at the People Also Ask box on the results page. Each question you click opens more. Write down the ones your existing content does not answer.

Google Trends

Google Trends does not give volumes, but it tells you whether interest is growing, falling or seasonal, and which related queries are rising. Use it to decide timing. A seasonal topic should be refreshed a few weeks before its annual peak, not during it.

The results page itself

For any query you are considering, search it. If the top results are forum threads, thin pages or articles from years ago, the competition is weaker than any tool will tell you. If the page is dominated by large brands with strong, recent guides, move on unless you have something genuinely different to add.

Why refresh pages ranking in positions 8 to 20 first?

A page with an average position between roughly 8 and 20 is on the edge of page one. Google has already judged it relevant. Small improvements can move it into positions where people click. In my experience at SEOGidi, this is the work with the best return for the least effort on almost every established site.

A note on the data: average position is an average across every query and every impression, so treat it as a filter to find candidates, not an exact ranking. Check the specific queries for each page.

  1. In Search Console, filter Pages by average position greater than 7.9 and less than 20.1. Sort by impressions.
  2. For each page, list its top ten queries. Search the main one yourself and read what ranks above you.
  3. Check intent first. If the top results are step-by-step guides and yours is an opinion piece, the format is the problem.
  4. Rewrite the opening so it answers the main question in the first two or three sentences.
  5. Add sections for the queries the page already gets impressions for but does not address.
  6. Remove outdated facts, update screenshots and examples, and add what you have learnt since you wrote it. Change the modified date only if you changed the substance.
  7. Improve the title tag so it matches the main query and gives a reason to click.
  8. Add three to five internal links to the page from related pages that already get traffic.
  9. Use URL Inspection to request indexing, note the date, and compare the next four to eight weeks against the previous period.

Do a handful of pages a month. Keep a log of what you changed. Over a year that log becomes the most useful SEO document your business owns.

How does internal linking create free traffic?

Internal links spread the authority your strongest pages have earned to the pages that need it, and they tell Google which pages matter. On many small sites, a few pages hold all the links and traffic, and everything else sits orphaned.

Find your top ten pages by clicks in Search Console. From each, add links to two or three related pages you want to rank, using descriptive anchor text that reflects the target page's main query. Then do the reverse: make sure every important page is reachable within a few clicks of the home page. This takes an afternoon and costs nothing.

Can programmatic pages bring free traffic without breaking Google's rules?

Yes, but this is where people get sites penalised. Programmatic pages are pages built from a template and a dataset: one page per town, per product integration, per job title. Done well, they answer real, specific searches that a single page cannot. Done badly, they are hundreds of near-identical pages with a word swapped.

Google draws the line in its spam policies. Since the March 2024 core update and spam policy changes, scaled content abuse covers generating many pages mainly to manipulate rankings rather than help users, however they are made: by AI, by people or by templates. Sites hit by it can lose visibility well beyond the offending pages.

My test before building any programmatic set:

  • Does each page contain information that is genuinely different from its siblings, such as real local data, real prices, real specifications or real reviews?
  • Would someone landing on one page from search find it more useful than your single general page?
  • Is there real search demand for each variation, visible in Search Console, autocomplete or the results page?
  • Would you be comfortable if a Google reviewer looked at twenty of them side by side?

If the answer to any of those is no, build fewer, better pages. Start with ten, measure, and grow only the pattern that earns clicks.

How to repurpose content for more reach

Every useful piece you publish can travel further without new research. A detailed guide can become a LinkedIn post with the single most surprising point, a short video answering the main question, a thread on the community where your customers ask about it, and a section in your newsletter. Each version should stand alone and link back only where the full piece adds something.

Repurposing also works in reverse. Questions you get in sales calls, comments and Reddit threads are content briefs. Answer them publicly once, then link to the answer every time the question comes up again. I cover the off-site channels in more detail in how to build an organic presence for free.

Why a newsletter is the traffic you own

Search traffic is rented from Google. AI Overviews and AI Mode answer more questions on the results page, and some informational clicks have fallen even for sites whose visibility held. A newsletter is the one channel where you decide when people hear from you.

Put a simple sign-up on your best-performing pages, offer something specific in return (a checklist, a monthly round-up of what changed in your field), and send it consistently. Most email platforms have a free tier that will carry a small list. Each issue sends returning visitors to your newest and refreshed pages, and returning visitors are the ones who convert.

Expect some of this traffic to show up in analytics as direct or unattributed. That is normal, and I explain why in the dark funnel.

Quick answers

How long does it take to get free organic traffic?
Refreshes of pages already ranking 8 to 20 often move within weeks. New content on a young site can take several months.

Do I need paid tools?
Not to start. Search Console, Google Trends, autocomplete and People Also Ask will find more opportunities than most small teams can act on.

How often should I publish?
Less often than you think. One strong new page and two careful refreshes a month will beat four rushed posts.

Is AI-written content safe to use?
Google judges the content, not the tool. Mass-producing pages to rank is scaled content abuse whoever writes them. Using AI to draft something you then check and improve is not.

Open Search Console today, filter your pages by position 8 to 20, and pick the three with the most impressions. Refresh those this month. That is the cheapest traffic you will ever earn.
