Basic SEO Techniques for Beginners: What to Do First, and Why It Works

By 'Wole Oduwole

Basic SEO comes down to three jobs. Make sure Google can find and index your pages. Make each page the clearest answer to one specific search. Give Google and real people reasons to trust you. Everything else in this guide, from keyword research to Core Web Vitals, is a way of doing one of those three jobs better.

I first wrote this guide in 2023. It sent readers to Google's Mobile-Friendly Test, which Google has since retired, and it said nothing about AI Overviews, because they did not exist in the UK yet. The fundamentals have held up. The tools and a few of the rules have not, so this is the version I would hand a new client or a junior on my team in November 2026.

How does Google search actually work?

Google does three things, in order, and most beginner problems come from not knowing which stage is failing.

  1. Crawling. Googlebot discovers URLs by following links and reading sitemaps, then fetches the pages.
  2. Indexing. Google renders the page, works out what it is about, picks a canonical version if there are duplicates, and decides whether to store it. Plenty of crawled pages are never indexed.
  3. Ranking. When someone searches, Google pulls candidates from the index and orders them by relevance, quality and usefulness for that query, in that location, on that device.

Google explains this in its own How Search works guide, and it is worth twenty minutes of your time. The practical lesson is simple. If a page is not indexed, no amount of keyword work will help it. If it is indexed but ranks nowhere, the problem is relevance or quality. Diagnose the stage before you fix anything.

How do I set up Google Search Console?

Search Console is the one free tool every site owner needs. It is the only place Google tells you directly what it sees: which queries you appear for, which pages are indexed, and what is broken.

  1. Go to Google Search Console and sign in with the Google account the business owns, not a freelancer's personal account.
  2. Add a Domain property rather than a URL-prefix property. It covers http, https, www and every subdomain in one view.
  3. Verify ownership by adding the TXT record Google gives you to your domain's DNS settings. Your registrar or host will have a DNS page for this.
  4. Submit your XML sitemap under Sitemaps. Most CMS platforms generate one at /sitemap.xml or /sitemap_index.xml.
  5. Add at least one other owner, so you never lose access when someone leaves.
  6. Wait a few days for data, then open the Performance report and the Pages report (under Indexing). Those two reports will answer most of your early questions.

Do the same in Bing Webmaster Tools. You can import your Search Console sites in a couple of clicks. Bing's index matters more than its market share suggests, because several AI assistants draw on it.

How do I choose the right keywords?

The old version of this post told you to look for keywords with high volume and moderate competition. That advice is fine as far as it goes, but it skips the part that decides whether you rank: intent.

Every search has a job behind it. Someone typing "chocolate cake recipe" wants to bake. Someone typing "birthday cake delivery Wolverhampton" wants to buy. Someone typing "why did my cake sink" wants a fix. Google has learnt what kind of page satisfies each of those, and it will not rank a product page for a recipe query however well you optimise it.

So before you write anything, search the query yourself and look at what ranks. Are the top results guides, product pages, local listings, videos or forum threads? That is Google telling you the format it believes answers the question. Match it, then do it better.

For a beginner, free tools are enough:

  • Google autocomplete and People Also Ask show you how real people phrase things.
  • Google Keyword Planner gives rough volume ranges. Treat them as ranges, not facts.
  • Search Console shows queries you already appear for. Once you have a few months of data, this becomes your best keyword tool.

Pick one main query per page, plus the close variations that share the same intent. If two of your pages target the same intent, merge them. I go further into finding winnable queries with free tools in my playbook on getting organic traffic for free.

How to write title tags, meta descriptions and headings

These are the first things a searcher sees and the clearest signals of what a page covers.

Title tags. Put the main query near the front, describe the page honestly, and keep it to roughly 50 to 60 characters so it is less likely to be cut off. Google rewrites titles it thinks are unhelpful or misleading, so a plain, accurate title usually survives better than a clever one.

Meta descriptions. They do not affect rankings directly, but they affect whether people click. Write one or two sentences that say what the reader gets. Google will often replace it with text from the page that better matches the query, which is fine.

Headings. Use one H1 that states the topic. Use H2s for the main sections, phrased the way people search where that reads naturally. A heading like "How long does a chocolate cake take to bake?" helps the reader scan and helps Google match the section to a question. Do not stuff headings with keywords. Write them for the person skimming on a phone.

Clean URLs help too. /chocolate-cake-recipe/ tells everyone what to expect. /p?id=4471 tells nobody anything.

Why do internal links matter so much?

Internal links are the most underused technique I see on small sites. They do three things at once. They help Googlebot discover pages. They tell Google which pages you consider important, because important pages get linked to more. And the anchor text tells Google what the target page is about.

A page with no internal links pointing to it is an orphan. Google may find it through your sitemap, but it will treat it as an afterthought. On client sites I work on, adding a handful of descriptive internal links from related, already-ranking pages is often the fastest win available, and it costs nothing.

Use descriptive anchors ("our guide to sourdough starters"), not "click here". Link from the body copy where it helps the reader, not only from menus and footers.

What technical SEO does a beginner actually need?

You do not need to become a developer. You do need to check a short list.

Indexing

Open the Pages report in Search Console. Look at the reasons pages are not indexed. "Crawled, currently not indexed" usually means Google did not think the page was worth keeping, which is a quality problem. "Excluded by noindex tag" on a page you want ranked is a mistake you can fix today. Use the URL Inspection tool to test any single page.

Sitemaps and robots.txt

Your sitemap should list only the canonical, indexable URLs you want found. Your robots.txt should not block anything you want ranked, including the CSS and JavaScript files Google needs to render the page. Remember that robots.txt stops crawling, not indexing. To keep a page out of results, use a noindex tag and let Google crawl it.

HTTPS and mobile

Serve every page over HTTPS. Google uses mobile-first indexing, which means it looks at the mobile version of your site. If content or links are missing on mobile, Google will not see them. Check your own site on a phone, and use Lighthouse in Chrome DevTools now that the standalone Mobile-Friendly Test has gone.

Core Web Vitals

Core Web Vitals are Google's measures of real-user experience. There are three, and one changed after the original version of this post. On 12 March 2024, Interaction to Next Paint (INP) replaced First Input Delay (FID). INP is stricter, because it measures how quickly the page responds to every interaction during a visit, not only the first one.

  • Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): how fast the main content appears. Good is 2.5 seconds or less.
  • Interaction to Next Paint (INP): how fast the page responds to taps, clicks and key presses. Good is 200 milliseconds or less.
  • Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): how much the layout jumps around. Good is 0.1 or less.

Check them in the Core Web Vitals report in Search Console or at PageSpeed Insights. Be realistic about their weight. They are part of the page experience signals Google uses, but a fast page with thin content will not beat a slower page that answers the question properly. Fix anything rated "Poor", then go back to content.

What does Google mean by helpful content and E-E-A-T?

The 2023 version of this post said "content is king" and pointed at a few famous blogs. That is not wrong, but it is not useful either. Here is what has changed.

In March 2024 Google folded its helpful content system into its core ranking systems and, in the same announcement, introduced new spam policies against scaled content abuse, site reputation abuse and expired domain abuse. The message was plain: pages made mainly to rank, rather than to help someone, are now a ranking liability across the whole site.

Google's guidance on helpful, reliable, people-first content is the best checklist available. The questions that matter most for a beginner:

  • Does the page show first-hand experience? Photos you took, results you got, mistakes you made.
  • Would someone leave feeling they learnt enough to act, or would they go back and search again?
  • Is it clear who wrote it and why they are qualified?
  • Did you add something that is not already on the ten pages ranking above you?

That last set of ideas is what Google calls E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust. It is not a score you can optimise. It is a description of what Google's systems try to reward. Show your work, name your authors, cite your sources and be accurate. For most small sites, trust is built more by what other people say about you than by anything on your own pages, which is why I wrote a separate guide on building organic presence for free beyond your website.

How do AI Overviews and AI assistants change the basics?

Less than people fear, more than people admit.

Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode draw on the same index as classic results. A page that is not indexed cannot be cited. A page that answers a question clearly, in a self-contained paragraph under a clear heading, is easier for any system to quote. So the basics above still do most of the work.

What changes is the click. More questions are answered on the results page, so some informational traffic falls even when your visibility holds. In June 2026 Google began rolling out generative AI performance reports in Search Console, which show impressions in AI Overviews and AI Mode. Access was still expanding when I wrote this, so check whether your property has it.

The other change is that assistants such as ChatGPT and Perplexity decide whether to mention you based on how consistently the wider web describes you, not only on your pages. That is entity work, and it is the next step after the basics. I cover it in entities, not keywords.

Quick answers

How long does basic SEO take to work?
New pages on a new site often take several months to settle. Fixes to indexing problems or titles on pages Google already trusts can show up within weeks.

Do I need paid SEO tools to start?
No. Search Console, Bing Webmaster Tools, PageSpeed Insights and a careful look at the results page will carry a beginner a long way.

Are backlinks still important?
Yes, but earned mentions from relevant, real sites matter far more than volume. Buying links breaks Google's spam policies and is not worth the risk.

Should I write content with AI?
Google does not penalise content for how it was made. It penalises content made mainly to manipulate rankings. If AI helps you draft, add the experience, checking and judgement only you can provide.

If you do one thing this week, set up Search Console properly and read the Pages report. Fix what is not indexed before you write another word. That single habit puts you ahead of most of the sites you are competing with.
